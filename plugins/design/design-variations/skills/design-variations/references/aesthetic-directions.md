# Aesthetic directions

Read this at step 3 for brand-new designs with no product theme to follow, and for the boldest options when the
user has unlocked the visual language; for those, read the whole file, because the library and the clichés are
where directions part. For redesigns and new pieces inside an existing product, read section 1 only: there the
product's theme governs.

## Contents

## 1. Fitting an existing theme

When a product exists, every option speaks its language: its colour roles, type families, spacing rhythm,
radii, elevation, iconography, imagery and voice. What varies is how boldly that language is used.

| Band | How far the visual language moves |
|---|---|
| Refine | Exactly the product's tokens; better hierarchy and rhythm |
| Evolve | The same tokens used more decisively: larger type contrast, stronger brand colour fields, a clearer grid |
| Rethink | The product's tokens on a new structure; new components drawn in the product's style |
| Reinvent | The brand at its boldest: scale, imagery, motion and composition can change, but colours, type families and voice remain recognisably the product unless the user lifted them |

How loud the product already is caps how loud its boldest option may be. A calm finance tool's boldest option
is confident, not neon. When the user lifts the theme ("ignore our current look"), treat the round as a new
design and use the library below.

## 2. Committing to a direction

A direction is a coherent set of choices, not a mood word. Before building a new-design option, write its
direction line: one line covering type, colour, shape, texture, motion and density. Keep every element of that
option faithful to it, and record the line in the option's `direction` field.

- **Type:** one to three families with a reason (a distinctive display face over a quiet text face is common).
- **Colour:** a ground, an ink and zero to two accents, with a dominant colour and a sharp accent rather than
  many evenly used colours. Meet contrast.
- **Light or dark:** chosen from the use scene (where, when and for how long people use it: a bright office, a
  dim room at night, a phone in sunlight), never from the category ("technical means dark"). Light reads better
  for most people in most settings; offer the other when people will want it.
- **Shape:** radius scale, line weights, and separation by borders, fills or space.
- **Texture and depth:** flat, layered, grainy, photographic or illustrated.
- **Motion:** none, subtle transitions, or one well-orchestrated moment.
- **Density:** compact, comfortable or airy.

Across the ten options of a new-design round, vary directions on purpose: light and dark, serif and sans,
restrained and expressive. Never let every option converge on the same safe look. Two tests, run on the
thumbnails rather than the direction names (a brief that fixes the look overrides them, section 4):

- **Any two options differ in at least two of** ground colour family (white or grey, warm paper, dark, a
  saturated colour, photography), type genre (neutral grotesk, geometric, humanist or rounded sans, serif,
  display, mono) and imagery treatment (none, icons, photography, illustration, material texture, diagrams).
  Neighbouring neutral directions (Swiss, Institutional plain, Data-ink minimal, Flat 2.0) on a white ground
  with a neutral grotesk are one look, however they are labelled: keep at most one of them per round.
- **When the brand is open, at least half the directions draw on the subject's own world:** its materials,
  objects, places and documents (for a pottery studio: clay, glaze, kiln marks, the studio's light; for a bike
  shop: the job ticket, the pegboard, the parts drawing). The rest may come from the library by fit.

Name the ground, type genre and imagery in each direction line, so the tests can be checked.

## 4. Matching directions to subjects

- Pick directions that fit the audience and the job; one or two Experimental options may stretch further to
  show what is possible.
- The Proven band uses the directions most expected for the category, executed exceptionally; Fresh and Novel
  bands use less expected ones that still fit; Experimental may use a direction that surprises.
- If someone could guess an option's direction from the product category alone, or from the category plus the
  clichés it avoids, choose again with the subject's own world in mind.
- Record each option's direction in its `direction` field, and avoid repeating one within a round unless the
  brief fixes it. Different names are not enough: run the two tests in section 2 on the built thumbnails.
- For redesigns, directions are not applied: options vary inside the theme on the axes (section 1). Only a
  rung 10 option with the theme lifted, or an explicit lift by the user, may change direction.
- A brief's own words always win: if the user said "minimal" or named a reference brand, every option honours
  it and the variation moves to structure, interaction and composition.

## 5. Clichés to avoid

These recur across independent design-quality guides and reviews of machine-made interfaces. Avoid them when the
axis is free. Banning them is not enough, because a ban only moves the default to the next cliché: choose a
direction positively from the library by fit, record it, then check the option against this list.

- indigo or violet gradient washes as backgrounds, and gradient text;
- one default typeface (often Inter) for everything, or any face picked by reflex rather than for a reason;
- identical rounded cards (icon, heading, line of text) with the same radius and the same soft grey shadow
  everywhere, and cards nested inside cards;
- a thick coloured stripe on the left or right edge of cards and alerts as the main accent;
- emoji or text symbols used as icons;
- a big-number hero metric with a small label and a gradient accent;
- tracked all-caps eyebrow labels over every section, middle-dot meta strings everywhere, and an arrow appended
  to every button;
- numbered 01/02/03 markers on things that are not sequences;
- fade-and-slide-up entrances on every section, and hover effects on every card;
- the three default "tasteful" looks: cream ground with a high-contrast serif and terracotta; near-black with
  a single acid or neon accent; broadsheet hairlines with no radius and dense columns;
- glass and blur as decoration, and hard offset shadows outside a genuinely neo-brutalist direction;
- monospace as a "technical" costume, and decorative sparklines or progress rings that carry no data;
- a centred hero with three feature boxes and a "Get started" button below, by default;
- light or dark chosen by category rather than by the use scene;
- invented statistics, testimonials, logos or ratings.

When a brand legitimately uses one of these (its colours are purple, its type is Inter), it is the brand, not a
cliché: keep it, and make the design distinctive through composition and hierarchy instead. The same holds for
the chrome of the user's own options canvas or gallery (for example its eyebrow labels or middle-dot meta lines):
it is theirs, so keep it as it is.
