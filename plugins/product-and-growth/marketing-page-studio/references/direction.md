# Page direction worksheet

Use this after product discovery, before layout. The worksheet is short enough to write directly into a project brief.

## Define the marketing decision

- Audience: who recognizes the problem immediately? Buyer: who controls the next step?
- Job and context: what are they trying to finish, with what constraint?
- Current friction: describe an observable situation rather than asserting an unsupported cost.
- Product action: which actual step changes that situation?
- Outcome: what can the viewer reasonably conclude from that step?
- Proof: claim IDs and capture IDs that support the promise.
- CTA: exact label, destination and prerequisite.
- Constraints: stack, privacy, accessibility, assets, legal/brand limits and scope.

If supplied materials support several benefits, select one primary story and leave deeper capabilities to feature pages. Do not ask the user to repeat facts already in the repository. Keep planned functionality in a future-facing section only when needed and clearly labeled.

## Build a narrative from questions

A useful order often moves from recognition to product action, evidence, fit and commitment. Treat that as a reasoning model, not a fixed section list. A developer tool may need code first; a familiar workflow may need a before/after; an AI product may need its input, review boundary and failure recovery visible before broad claims.

For each planned section, write the visitor's question, the exact claim, the product material that answers it, and the next action. Remove sections with no distinct job. Vary density and composition only as needed to help the reader.

## Choose a visual direction

Inspect the actual product's interface and brand. Write two plausible directions with different hierarchy and product framing; select the one that makes its value easiest to see. This is a quick creative decision, not a mandatory approval stage.

Examples of direction decisions:

| Situation | Useful composition | Risk to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A dense dashboard contains one persuasive transformation | Large complete detail paired with a small contextual view | Shrinking the entire dashboard into decoration |
| A short workflow is understandable through interaction | Working bounded surface near the promise | Decorative controls with no state model |
| The product has a distinctive visual output | Actual output as the hero material, with a clear input/result relation | Generic invented assets implying product output |
| A buyer needs trustworthy operational detail | Restrained hierarchy, explicit limitations and sourced proof | Unsupported superlatives or borrowed customer logos |

The memorable interaction should reveal a benefit: a queue resolves into understandable groups; a before/after exposes a change; a workflow can be replayed and inspected. A particle field or repeated reveal adds little when the product itself is visually compelling.

## Redesign scope

Record the existing stack, routes, navigation, CTA destinations, analytics dependencies and working controls before editing. Preserve unrelated application behavior. Compare the before/after at the same widths and states. A stronger hero with broken navigation or hidden product detail is an incomplete redesign.

## Direction acceptance

A first-time visitor can answer what the product does, whom it serves, what changed and where to go next. Proof supports the promise. The CTA remains understandable with motion disabled. Assumptions and evidence gaps are explicit. Nothing depends on borrowed upstream branding or demo metrics.
