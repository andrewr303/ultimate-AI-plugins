# Editorial patterns to inspect

A pattern match is a reason to read a sentence. It is not a verdict about quality or authorship. Diagnose the reader's problem, check the context, and make the smallest useful edit. The examples are synthetic.

## Diagnose in order

Fix the largest problem first, because surface edits on a hollow or inaccurate draft only make it fluent:

1. **Truth and scope.** Inventory claims, attribution, claim state, uncertainty, numbers, quotations, links, conditions, and required structure. These are protected.
2. **Substance.** Find the real point and, in authored work, the material only Andrew supplied. If it is missing, mark a TK or ask (see `interview.md`).
3. **Development.** Make paragraphs depend on one another through cause, contrast, sequence, qualification, example, or consequence.
4. **Sentences.** Remove inflated, repetitive, or formulaic machinery while keeping useful voice and channel conventions.
5. **Craft.** Restore evidence, stance, warmth, or rhythm if the edit made the piece colder (see `longform.md`).

## Triage a flagged phrase

Ask in order, and leave the phrase alone if it survives all five:

1. Does cutting it lose meaning? If yes, it may be content; keep it or reword without losing the meaning.
2. Is it formula or choice? Could Andrew defend it if asked, or did a pattern put it there?
3. Does it announce rather than deliver? Commentary about the writing is almost always cuttable.
4. Does it add heat or light? Cut heat (intensifiers, flourishes); keep light (facts, mechanisms, limits).
5. Would a skeptical reader trust the sentence more or less without it?

Three tests make questions 2 and 4 concrete:

- **Flatten test:** state the claim without its cadence. If nothing specific remains, the formula was standing in for missing reasoning.
- **Relation test:** restate an implied connection with because, although, when, if, which, or so. If that requires inventing the relation, the original only suggested one.
- **Emphasis test:** delete the importance word. If the remaining sentence names an actor, mechanism, result, or limit, state that and let the reader judge; if it names nothing, the emphasis was the whole claim.

## How much weight a pattern carries

A habit deserves attention in proportion to how rarely a careful writer would choose it on purpose. Staged contrast, a slogan closer, a promised revelation, an objection nobody raised, assistant framing, and an open placeholder are worth a close read on one sighting. A hedge, a transition word, passive voice, a bold label, a three-item list, and a long word are ordinary; look only when several gather or one repeats until the reader notices. Read paragraph shape too: a contrast split across two sentences, or the same closer after every section, is the sentence habit at a larger scale.

## Generic opening

**Inspect:** "In today's rapidly changing landscape, communication is more important than ever."

The opening delays the specific point. Replace it with the supplied observation, action, or question. Do not invent an event to create a stronger hook.

**Keep when useful:** a brief greeting, a specific acknowledgment, an apology accepting responsibility, or a sentence that places an unfamiliar issue in context. "Thanks for checking the export" serves a relationship; repeating thanks three times may not.

## Meta throat-clearing

**Inspect:** "There is a failure mode worth naming directly, because well-intentioned teams walk straight into it. It is the belief that safety means a human approving every step."

The frame announces the point instead of making it. Delete it and state the thing: "Many teams equate safety with a human approving every step." Other frames: "Here is the tension," "It is worth noting that," "The key point is."

**Keep when useful:** navigation the reader needs in a long document, such as "Two caveats apply to the pilot numbers."

## Decorative contrast

**Inspect:** "This isn't just a dashboard. It's a revolution in decision-making."

The reversal adds significance without explaining behavior. Describe the concrete capability if it is known, or delete the claim. Correlative scaffolding ("not only X but also Y") inflates the same way; split or simplify it.

**Keep when useful:** "The limit applies per account, not per user." This corrects a real scope error. One earned contrast is rhetoric; one in every paragraph is a template. Do not delete genuine alternatives, refusals, exceptions, or comparisons because a regex sees negation. Never replace a contrast with a fabricated causal link.

## Unexplained significance or magnitude

**Inspect:** "The update is transformative and unlocks unprecedented value." Also importance-flagging ("Speed is not a footnote here." "This matters." "Make no mistake.") and grandiose prediction ("will define the next decade," "changes everything").

These tell the reader what to feel instead of showing the consequence. Ask what changed, for whom, and what evidence supports the effect, then apply the emphasis test. If no evidence exists, remove or narrow the claim. A supported mechanism can be enough; not every claim needs a statistic.

**Keep when useful:** a subjective opinion owned as an opinion, or a domain term such as "robust standard errors." "Significant" can have a defined statistical meaning. An evaluative word belongs where the same passage supplies the evidence that earns it. Do not substitute a stronger synonym just to evade a word list.

## Vague attribution and false precision

**Inspect:** "Studies show founders who raise early grow faster." "Experts warn the market is cooling."

An unnamed authority borrows credibility the draft cannot show. Name the supplied source, keep its uncertainty, ask for it, or cut the claim. Do not convert association into causation, "may" into "will," "some" into "most," or a vendor's claim into narrator fact. A more confident sentence is not automatically a clearer one.

**Keep when useful:** a named, dated source with its own hedging intact: "The vendor's March survey of 40 customers suggests renewals slowed."

## Abstract actors

**Inspect:** "The data tells us to slow down." "The market rewards speed." "The decision emerged from the review."

Data does not speak and decisions do not decide. Name the person, team, system, or measurement when that makes the action clearer: "Churn rose 4 points after the price change, so I'd slow the rollout."

**Keep when useful:** passive voice or an abstract subject when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, deliberately protected, or less important than the object. "The data shows a 12% drop" reports a measurement and is fine.

## Staged insight and rhetorical ceremony

**Inspect:** "Here's what everyone misses. The real lesson? Alignment."

State the actual point if one exists. A colon or rhetorical question is not inherently defective; the problem is a setup that promises more than its content delivers.

**Keep when useful:** a real question the recipient must answer, a heading that helps navigation, or a colon introducing an actual explanation, quotation, or list.

## Demonstrative kicker

**Inspect:** "...reuse the approval flow we already have. That instinct backfires. Four constraints break the assumption." Others: "This is where the risk hides." "That is the whole point."

A short "This/That + verdict" sentence after a full one usually restates the sentence before it. Cut it, or fold the verdict into the previous sentence and pivot with a real transition: "...reuse the approval flow we already have, but four constraints break that assumption."

**Keep when useful:** a conversational acknowledgment ("That works for me.") or a short beat Andrew would defend because it lands a point the paragraph built.

## Decorative metaphor

**Inspect:** "an authorization bug wearing the costume of a performance optimization."

The image performs wit instead of stating the relation. Say it plainly: "an authorization bug, not a performance optimization."

**Keep when useful:** an image that carries meaning the plain version loses, especially in a requested narrative or a personal note.

## Hedged confidence and false balance

**Inspect:** "One might argue..." "In many ways..." "It's important to consider..." and "On one hand... on the other hand..." when Andrew has a view, or a both-sides wrap-up on a question the piece already answered.

The sentence sounds careful while committing to nothing. State the position and give the strongest real objection its own space.

**Keep when useful:** genuine uncertainty that affects the decision, stated once where it governs the claim.

## Objection nobody raised

**Inspect:** "I'm not saying documentation doesn't matter. Don't get me wrong, process has its place. But the question is whether the agent can use the instruction."

The first two sentences answer a position that appears nowhere else. Make the claim the denials were protecting.

**Keep when useful:** an objection the text attributes or answers in full, an option the reader would really weigh, or a stated limit ("this doesn't cover refunds").

## Leftovers and the wrong reader

**Inspect:** "Great question!" or "I hope this helps" in a standalone document; "details are not publicly available"; an unfilled bracket or citation markup; a reply that restates the problem the recipient wrote and reaches the decision in its last line; "The table below compares the three vendors."

Assistant framing addresses someone who is not the reader. A stated gap presents missing information as information: say what the source does not show, or cut it. In a reply, lead with the decision and keep only the reasoning that would change whether the reader agrees. Say the thing in place of describing the page.

**Keep when useful:** a sincere greeting or sign-off, an attributed estimate, a `[TK]` Andrew still owes, background for a reader new to the thread.

## Repeated framing and synonym cycling

**Inspect:** a product becomes a platform, solution, ecosystem, engine, and partner in successive sentences without a change in referent.

Choose the accurate name and repeat it when that is clearer. Avoid a string of interchangeable conclusions such as "This matters," "That is the key," and "The distinction is important."

**Keep when useful:** different terms that genuinely distinguish objects. Do not collapse a customer, account, workspace, and user into one label for stylistic consistency.

## Inflated completion or causation

**Inspect:** "The test proves the launch will succeed." A successful test may establish only that one scenario worked.

Restore the scope supported by the source. Keep association, chronology, hypothesis, prediction, and demonstrated causation distinct. Preserve who observed what and under which conditions.

**Keep when useful:** a causal explanation supported by supplied evidence. A connector such as "because" must reflect an established relationship, not a rhythm repair.

## Chopped reasoning

**Inspect:** "The preview works. The export fails. Labels disappear. We cannot review it."

If supported: "The preview works, but the export drops the labels, so we can't use it for the review." Run the relation test first and join only the thoughts whose relationship is established.

**Keep when useful:** "Tuesday works. I'll send the file that morning." The reply is complete. Do not add subordinate clauses, parentheses, or a long sentence to reach a cadence score. A list can be better than joining independent questions.

## Overloaded sentence

**Inspect:** a sentence that combines the decision, three conditions, two unrelated examples, and an aside about history.

Keep each condition next to the action it qualifies. Split at a genuine decision boundary or use a list. Do not assume that longer syntax is more authentic, or that a low reading grade is automatically better.

**Keep when useful:** a long sentence whose structure lets the reader follow one connected argument. Length by itself is not an error.

## Repetitive architecture

**Inspect:** every paragraph starts with a verdict, contains exactly three claims, and ends with a short slogan. Related shapes: every section opens with a definition ("X is a..."), every example is followed by its interpretation, every list is padded to three items, bullets carry an argument that needs connected prose, and bold is scattered until nothing stands out.

Let the thought choose its shape. Fix only the dominant problem: move or combine material, let one section carry more weight, name the relation at a weak join, or stop at the last concrete consequence. Remove repeated explanations before adjusting rhythm. Do not repair uniformity by mechanically alternating long and short sentences or adding a random tangent.

**Keep when useful:** parallel headings, a checklist, numbered steps, or a three-item list containing exactly three necessary things ("verify the certificate, the DNS record, and the health check"). Keep two items when two is true. Required structure is not filler.

## Empty close

**Inspect:** "The future belongs to those who embrace the change." Also a paragraph-closing sentence that restates what the paragraph just said.

End on the supported implication or next action already in the piece. Delete an empty kicker rather than replacing it with a more polished metaphor.

**Keep when useful:** an actual decision, a necessary question, or "Let me know which slot works" when the recipient is choosing a time. A short confirmation may need no additional CTA. Do not force exactly one question when coordination requires several answers.

## Excess reassurance or performative empathy

**Inspect:** several sentences assuring the reader that everything is understandable without addressing the problem.

Keep the useful acknowledgment, then answer the concern or name the action. Preserve accountability and any intended boundary. Do not turn an apology into a denial or a refusal into enthusiasm.

**Keep when useful:** a specific thank-you, a sincere apology tied to an action, or an acknowledgment of a concrete difficulty. Warmth can do real work.

## Invented specificity and specificity loss

**Inspect:** adding "in 30 seconds," "our largest customer," "last Tuesday," or "I learned this firsthand" when no source supplies it. Also inspect edits that turn "estimated at least 12%" into "significantly," and specific-looking vagueness such as "a vendor once caused a billing problem," which has the grammar of an example and the content of an abstraction.

Restore the source. Protect numbers, units, denominators, time windows, uncertainty, attribution, and whether a statement is an observation, target, promise, or opinion. For a vague example, ask which vendor, what happened, and how it was caught; if the source does not say, use a TK or cut it. Do not add an emotional reaction or biography to make a piece feel human.

**Keep when useful:** supported specifics and a clearly marked placeholder. A qualitative statement is acceptable when the source does not justify a number.

## The same pattern under new punctuation

**Inspect:** a rewrite that removes a dash and keeps the move: "It's not a feature — it's a platform" becoming "It's not a feature: it's a platform" or "It's not a feature. It's a platform." A colon reveal that replaces a dashed reveal, or a period that turns one staged contrast into two short sentences, is the same pattern.

Andrew's no-dash default makes this the most common false fix. Repair the claim (state what the platform does) rather than the punctuation.

## Portability test, used carefully

If a sentence or paragraph could move unchanged to almost any company or author, inspect whether it contributes meaning. Make it specific only when supplied facts allow that. Otherwise cut unnecessary framing.

This is not a ban on ordinary coordination language. "Does Tuesday work?" can be entirely useful without a product name, metric, mechanism, or company reference.

## Plainer words

Not banned, only flagged for scrutiny. If the plain word is worse, keep the original. `scripts/lint.py` names these alternatives in its findings.

| Flagged | Plainer choice |
|---|---|
| delve into | look at, dig into |
| tapestry, landscape, realm | cut, or name the actual thing |
| underscore, highlight | show |
| leverage (verb), utilize | use |
| seamless, frictionless | easy, or say how |
| robust | reliable, or say how |
| crucial, pivotal, vital | important, or say why |
| navigate (figurative) | handle |
| foster, cultivate | build |
| elevate, unlock, empower | improve, enable, let |
| myriad, plethora | many, or the number |
| prior to, subsequent to | before, after |
| is able to, has the ability to | can |
| game-changer, revolutionary | the concrete change |

## What to keep

Removing these is the over-correction failure, because they are voice rather than formula:

- an earned fragment: a short beat after a long sentence, used sparingly;
- deliberate parallelism that makes a real distinction ("You can have either. You cannot have both.");
- a closing line the preceding material earned;
- first-person conviction where Andrew owns a real position ("I think the pricing is wrong");
- a real aside, self-correction, mixed feeling, or unresolved doubt;
- deliberate repetition of the right ordinary word, trade language, or an unusual checkable detail.

The test is whether Andrew could defend the choice if asked. Do not insert any of these as "human signals"; existing texture can be preserved, but invented texture is performance.

## Calibrate the touch

- **Light touch** for a personal note, a reply in an established thread, or a draft that already sounds like him: fix clear formula and lexicon, protect voice.
- **Heavier touch** for marketing, launch, and proposal copy, which tends to collect stock language: still surgical, but more candidates will be real.
- **Match register:** a playful reply stays playful and an investor memo stays measured. Do not neutralize every piece toward a generic middle.

## Stated avoid list

Andrew's standing instructions name words to avoid in newly authored prose: delve, tapestry, landscape (figurative), vibrant, pivotal, crucial, foster, bolster, garner, underscore, nuanced, multifaceted, intricate, meticulous, leverage (as a verb), harness, illuminate, realm, robust, holistic, seamless, streamlined, nestled, renowned, groundbreaking, comprehensive, moreover, furthermore, additionally (as an opener), encompassing, cultivating, showcasing, embodies, epitomizes, spearheading, interplay, indelible, endeavor, advent. When one appears, repair the meaning or cut the sentence; a synonym swap does not fix an empty claim. Leave literal technical terms, official names, quotations, and code alone.

## Formatting preferences

Business defaults inherited from the previous skill favor straight quotes and alternatives to em/en dashes. These are preferences, not evidence of authorship. A current user request, required house style, faithful quotation, proper name, URL, code, mathematical notation, or technical identifier can justify an exception.

A scanner can mask some source spans, but masking is heuristic. Inspect exact quotations and code manually before changing them. A clean scan cannot establish fact preservation.

## Assessing a passage

Use the format in `review.md`: piece-level diagnosis first, then one block per material issue with a keep, revise, ask-author, or cut verdict. Quote enough text for the reader to locate the issue, name the consequence, and suggest a concrete move. Avoid listing harmless matches simply to make a report look thorough. Group overlapping findings so one sentence does not appear to have several independent defects because several patterns matched it. Order findings by what they cost the reader: credibility first (a changed or unsupported claim, an open placeholder, assistant framing), then clarity, then polish, and say which are clear problems and which are judgment calls.

If asked whether AI wrote the passage, separate that question from editing: style alone does not establish authorship. Report what the reader can inspect and leave origin unclaimed.
