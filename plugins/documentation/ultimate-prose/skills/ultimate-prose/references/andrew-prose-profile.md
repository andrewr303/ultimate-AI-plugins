# Andrew prose profile, version 4.5

This profile describes how Andrew writes in two registers, from a local review of 85 sent emails (professional) and 93 posts on X (casual). `corpus-notes.md` records the method, the counts, and the limits. The numbers describe that sample. None is a quota, and nothing here should be added to a piece only to make it look like him.

Every example below is synthetic. None reproduces a private message, a client's name, or a real project detail.

## The voice in both registers

- **Answer first.** The first words do the job: yes or no, the status, the reaction.
- **First person and owned.** He writes "I think", "I'm confident", "in my opinion", and says what he did and what he will do next. About a quarter of his email sentences open with "I", "I'm", "I'll", "I've", or "I'd".
- **Calibrated.** Confidence is stated with its limit: "should", "pretty confident", "fairly confident", "I'm guessing", "probably", then the check he still wants to run.
- **Warm and accommodating, with a line.** He says he is happy to help and means it, then names what sits outside the agreed scope. When a brief has him agreeing to something, taking on work, or trying a thing, write that warmth in ("Sounds good", "happy to", "excited to try it"); a neutral statement of the same fact reads colder than he is.
- **Plain accountability.** When he gets something wrong he says so in one or two sentences, usually with a short apology ("I'm sorry I missed the call"), and states what he will do differently. No excuses and no grovelling.
- **Concrete.** Dates, counts, file names, version numbers, and side-by-side figures appear wherever they exist. A comparison names both sides.
- **Finance lens.** Cost, margin, incentive, ownership, and risk are his natural frames. He mentions his finance background when it earns the point, and only then.
- **Curious and forward-looking.** He often ends on what he will try, check, or watch next.
- **Plain joins.** Sentences connect with "and", "but", "so", "because", "though", and "which". Semicolons, em dashes, and en dashes are close to absent in both registers.

## Professional register: email

### Shape follows length

Half of his emails are 60 words or fewer, and those read differently from the long ones.

**Quick reply (60 words or fewer).** Usually no greeting. The answer comes in the first two or three words, then at most one follow-on sentence or question, then the sign-off. Typical openers: "Yes", "Yes, that works", "Sounds good", "Confirmed", "Thank you!", "No problem", "Agreed". An exclamation mark appears here more than anywhere else, one at most, to carry goodwill.

> Sounds good, I'll send the revised file Thursday morning.
>
> Best regards,
> Andrew Rodriguez

**Longer email (over 60 words).** A greeting line, then the status or answer, then short paragraphs. Two sentences per paragraph is typical and more than four is rare. Sentences average about 19 to 20 words. When one paragraph is for one person in a group thread, he opens it with their name ("Dana, once the draft is in, I'd like to check the totals against the source file."). He ends on the next step or one specific question, with no summary.

**Sign-off.** "Best regards," and then "Andrew Rodriguez" on the next line, on almost every email, including two-word replies. A request sent to a client sometimes ends "Thanks," in place of it.

**Greeting.** "Hi Dana," is the default. "Hi everyone," or "Hi all," for a group. A bare first name ("Dana,") or "Hey Dana," once the relationship is established. He does not open with a wish for the recipient's wellbeing after the first introduction. If no name is supplied, skip the greeting line; a bare "Hi," is not his.

### Words and phrases he reaches for

Use these only where they fit the sentence, never several at once: "sounds good", "good to go", "in good shape", "shouldn't be an issue", "in a pinch", "in the same ballpark", "I figured", "I'm guessing", "either way", "thankfully", "right now", "on my end", "when you get a chance", "let me know if", "I'll keep you posted", "a second set of eyes", "a sanity check", "happy to", "I'd appreciate". "Also," often opens a sentence that adds one more item, and "And" or "But" opens a sentence now and then.

Contractions are constant: about one word in thirty. A long email with none reads stiff and unlike him.

### Texture to keep, never to manufacture

His quick replies are typed fast, often on a phone. They join clauses with a comma ("It looks good, I just noticed a few small issues"), drop the subject ("Going to work through that today."), skip the comma after "Yes", and lowercase tool names. When lightly editing his own draft, leave these alone unless he asks for polish or the message is formal. When drafting for him, write clean sentences in the same plain rhythm. Do not add comma splices, dropped subjects, or typos to look authentic.

### Client-facing versus internal

To a client he is shorter and a little more formal: fewer contractions, the answer, then one precise question or the next step. To his own team he is more candid about scope, timing, and frustration, always in measured terms and always paired with what he will do.

## Casual register: X

### Which rows are his words

The post database records how each post was made. Rows marked `Custom`, and the sentences a note says he added, are his own wording. An unedited drafted option that he picked shows what he approved; treat it as a preference, not as his phrasing. The two differ measurably: his own text has about twice the first-person density and many times the plain emphasis of the drafted options.

### What his own hand adds

- **A personal line, usually last.** A reaction and an intent ("Excited to try this, looks very promising."), a stance marked as his ("More people building is a good thing in my opinion."), or curiosity about what comes next ("Curious to see how this plays out.").
- **Plain enthusiasm.** "Really", "very", "pretty", "genuinely", "huge", "awesome", "excited", "blown away", "crushing it", "gamechanger" (one word). He says a thing is great when he thinks so.
- **Builder's standing, briefly.** "As a solo founder", "Coming from finance into engineering", then a concrete detail from his own work.
- **Agreement or pushback up front.** "Agree 100%." "Both, and that's fine." "I'd push back a little on that."
- **Parenthetical asides**, sometimes with an exclamation mark: "the first hundred customers (or a thousand!)".
- **Loose mechanics.** Unhyphenated modifiers ("speed wise", "equity based"), "+" and "=" as joins ("founder+engineer"), a dropped subject, a comma join, an occasional "lol", a closing "Thanks for sharing!".

### What he does not do

No em or en dashes, no emoji, and no hashtags in any of the 93 posts. No threads. On his own he rarely writes an aphoristic kicker or a slogan closer: the clever one-liner in his posts usually came from a drafted option, and his own endings are earnest.

### Shape

A reply runs about 50 words in two to four sentences of roughly 14 words. The usual order is the analytical point first, often framed through cost, margin, incentives, or ownership, and then his own line. A fact from his own work is not yet that line: add how he feels about it or what he expects, in plain words ("it's made a big difference", "curious to see how it holds up", "pretty impressed so far"). Vary the wording; these are examples, not stock lines. A reaction alone is fine ("Huge, excited to try"). He mentions what he is building only when the thread is about that subject, phrased as what he is building it around.

> Pricing is the part I'd watch. A flat fee works while usage is predictable, and it breaks the first month a big customer triples their volume. I've been testing usage-based billing lately and I'm pretty impressed so far.

## Cadence and relationships

Preserve connected reasoning. Put a condition next to its consequence, say what a comparison compares, and make the referent of "this" clear.

- If several short sentences only list effects, join the ones that share a supported relation.
- If a long sentence hides the decision, separate context from the action.
- If a brief reply already answers the question, leave it brief.
- If several technical questions need separate answers, use a list.

His longer emails average about 19 to 20 words a sentence, which agrees with the 17 to 24 range in his standing instructions. Both are descriptions to check a draft against. Neither is a target, and a quick reply at 14 words a sentence is equally his.

## Formatting defaults

Straight quotes and no em or en dashes in newly authored prose. The samples agree: dashes are almost absent, and the curly apostrophes in some emails come from a phone's mail client. He uses the serial comma about four times in five. Apply a live request first, and leave code, identifiers, exact quotations, official names, and URLs untouched.

## What the samples cannot establish

- Unassisted authorship of any sent email. The longer, more formal emails may have been drafted with help, so the quick replies and his noted additions to posts are the strongest evidence of unedited phrasing.
- His voice outside this context. The emails come from one client project over two months, and the posts are almost all replies. Essays, investor updates, cold outreach, and personal messages are not sampled here.
- A current fact sheet. Nothing in the samples is a fact to reuse in a new piece.
- Which wording caused engagement. Every rating field is blank.
- That any expression, punctuation mark, or scanner match identifies who or what wrote a text.

Context boundaries (who is speaking, which domain, how current a fact is) are in `personalization.md`, and the reasoning cues worth preserving are in `voice-calibration.md`.

When a new correction arrives, update the narrow rule it changes. Prefer explicit feedback and comparable examples to more global rules.
