# Co-write interview

Use when the user asks to co-write or to be interviewed, or asks for an authored piece (essay, article, newsletter, talk, personal story, opinion piece, practitioner post) whose value depends on their own experience or judgment while the brief supplies none of it. The goal is to get the author's own language and material onto the page, not to simulate a voice. Routine writing built on supplied facts, such as an email, an update, documentation, or product copy, does not need an interview.

## Ask once, then wait

Ask for one untidied answer before drafting:

> Talk this through in one take, out loud or typed, without organizing it first. Tell me what happened, why you want to write it now, who you want to reach, what you believe, and the examples or doubts that make it yours. Use real names, numbers, and incidents only if you are comfortable publishing them, and skip private details instead of blurring them.

Offer a few of these prompts as aids, not as a questionnaire to complete:

- What happened recently that made you want to write this: a conversation, a result, a failure, an annoyance?
- Picture one reader. What do they already know, and what should change for them afterward?
- Who disagrees with you, and what is their strongest real argument?
- What do you say about this in conversation that you have never written down?
- Which example from your own work carries the point? What happened, and what changed?
- Where are you unsure, or what have you changed your mind about?
- What would you cut from the conventional version of this piece?

Also ask for anything the author already wrote on the topic, such as posts, notes, talks, or lists, and treat it as source text with its attribution intact.

Do not draft before the answer arrives. If the user declines or wants a draft now, write only what the brief supports, mark each gap with `[TK: specific question]`, and say in one line what the draft lacks.

## When a draft already exists

Do not interview the author about the whole draft. Ask at most three questions about the parts that lack support or authorship:

```text
This paragraph says [claim]. What is your evidence, and how sure are you?
The example could belong to anyone. What is your version of it?
The ending restates the point. What should the reader do, notice, or reconsider?
```

One concrete follow-up usually yields more than five general questions: "Tell me about the last time this went wrong. What broke, and what did you do?"

## Turn the answer into prose

1. Extract the point, usable phrases, examples, uncertainties, and the order in which the author discovered the idea.
2. Build the spine from the author's language. Cut true repetition and remarks addressed to you, and reorder only when that clarifies the development.
3. Edit grammar lightly where comprehension needs it. Protect coined phrases, trade language, real asides, mixed feelings, and unusual details. If a stronger edit would change the thought or erase a distinctive phrase, keep the original or show the author both.
4. Add model-written material only for a stated job: sourced research, a definition, a factual bridge, or compression. Never write a memory, preference, or experience for the author.
5. Leave `[TK: precise question]` wherever the piece needs material the interview did not provide. A plain true sentence is better than a vivid invented one.
6. After the draft, outside the publishable text, add a short provenance note:

```text
Author material:     which sections or phrases came from the interview or prior writing
Model contribution:  research, structure, connective prose, or none
Open items:          the [TK] questions, or none
```

For a named file, put the note in the reply rather than the file. The note describes the actual process; never offer a detector result as proof of authorship.
