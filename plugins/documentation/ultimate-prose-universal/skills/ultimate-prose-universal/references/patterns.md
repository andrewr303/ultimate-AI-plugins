# Contextual editing patterns

These are reasons to inspect a passage, not universal bans or signs that establish who wrote it. Find the reader's problem and repair that problem. A phrase's mere presence does not justify an edit. All examples here are synthetic.

## Diagnose in order

Fix the largest problem first, because surface edits on a hollow or inaccurate draft only make it fluent:

1. **Truth and scope:** claims, attribution, uncertainty, numbers, quotations, links, conditions, and required structure are protected.
2. **Substance:** find the real point and, in authored work, the material only this author supplied. If it is missing, ask or mark a `[TK]` (see `interview.md`).
3. **Development:** make paragraphs depend on one another through cause, contrast, sequence, qualification, example, or consequence.
4. **Sentences:** remove inflated, repetitive, or formulaic machinery while keeping useful voice and medium conventions.
5. **Craft:** restore image, stance, warmth, or rhythm if the edit made the piece colder (see `longform.md`).

## Triage a flagged phrase

Ask in order, and leave the phrase alone if it survives all five:

1. Does cutting it lose meaning? If yes, it may be content; keep it or reword without losing the meaning.
2. Is it formula or choice? Could the author defend it if asked, or did a pattern put it there?
3. Does it announce rather than deliver? Commentary about the writing is almost always cuttable.
4. Does it add heat or light? Cut heat (intensifiers, flourishes); keep light (facts, mechanisms, limits).
5. Would a skeptical reader trust the sentence more or less without it?

Three tests make questions 2 and 4 concrete:

- **Flatten test:** state the claim without its cadence. If nothing specific remains, the formula was standing in for missing reasoning.
- **Relation test:** restate an implied connection with because, although, when, if, which, or so. If that requires inventing the relation, the original only suggested one.
- **Emphasis test:** delete the importance word. If the remaining sentence names an actor, mechanism, result, or limit, state that and let the reader judge; if it names nothing, the emphasis was the whole claim.
- **Slot-fill test:** if any word could fill the blank and the phrase would sound the same ("a [adjective] step forward for [noun]"), it says nothing about this subject.
- **Third-item test:** drop the last item of a list of three and ask what the reader loses. One natural trio always passes.
- **"What exactly?"** Ask it of familiar shorthand such as "capacity", "support", "reporting", or "implementation".

A failed test never licenses an invented detail: cut the sentence or mark a `[TK]`.

## How much weight a pattern carries

A habit deserves attention in proportion to how rarely a careful writer would choose it on purpose. Staged contrast, a slogan closer, a promised revelation, an objection nobody raised, assistant framing, and an open placeholder are worth a close read on one sighting. A dash, a hedge, passive voice, a transition word, a bold label, a three-item list, and a long word are ordinary; look only when several gather in one passage or one repeats until the reader notices. Correct grammar, a formal register, and a tidy structure are not evidence of anything.

Read paragraph shape as well as sentences: a contrast split across two sentences, three parallel examples, or the same closer after every section is the sentence-level habit at a larger scale. A match is a candidate, not a finding; `preservation.md` gives the steps from candidate to edit.

## Pattern table

| Pattern | Reader problem | Useful move | Legitimate counterexample |
|---|---|---|---|
| Generic opening: "In today's fast-paced world…" | Delays this piece's subject | Start with the actual observation, question, scene, or action | Brief orientation for an unfamiliar reader; an intentional parody |
| Meta throat-clearing: "There is a failure mode worth naming," "Here is the tension" | Announces the point instead of making it | Delete the frame and state the thing | Navigation a long document needs: "Two caveats apply to these numbers" |
| Inflated significance: "a revolutionary leap," "This matters," "will define the next decade" | Tells the reader what to feel instead of earning it | Apply the emphasis test; show the consequence or remove the claim | An owned opinion, supported historic description, or quoted claim |
| Vague attribution: "Studies show," "Experts warn" | Borrows authority the text cannot show | Name the supplied source with its hedging, ask for it, or cut | A named, dated source cited nearby |
| Significance bolted on with -ing: "…, highlighting the team's commitment" | Adds a comment where a fact was expected | End the sentence, or state the concrete consequence | "…, creating a second invoice for every order" is a real result |
| Vague association: "is associated with," "linked to" | Hides the actual relationship | State the relationship if the source gives it | The source gives nothing more: keep the vague wording |
| Sales register: "world-class," "seamless," "built with care" | Could sit on a competitor's page unchanged | Name the capability and who it helps | An award or measurement that is actually supplied |
| Abstract actor: "The data tells us," "The market rewards" | Hides who or what acts | Name the person, team, system, or measurement | Unknown or irrelevant actors; "The data shows a 12% drop" reports a measurement |
| Decorative contrast: "Not just a tool. A movement." Also split across sentences ("This doesn't mean X. It means Y.") | Denies a view nobody holds to add drama | Name what changes in practice | "The limit applies per account, not per person" corrects real scope |
| Objection nobody raised: "I'm not saying…", "Don't get me wrong", "One might be tempted to…" | Spends attention on a position that appears nowhere else | Make the claim the denial was protecting | An objection the text attributes or answers in full; an option a reader would really weigh |
| Correlative or hedged scaffolding: "not only… but also," "One might argue," "In many ways" | Inflates a simple sentence or commits to nothing | Split, simplify, or take the position | Genuine uncertainty that affects the decision, stated once |
| Staged insight: "Here's what nobody tells you" | Performs a reveal without earning it | State the actual point and evidence | A character's dialogue, a deliberately comic setup, or an actual question |
| Demonstrative kicker: "That instinct backfires." "This is where the risk hides." | Restates the previous sentence as a verdict | Cut it or fold it into the sentence before, then pivot with a real transition | "That works for me" in a reply; a beat the paragraph earned |
| Decorative metaphor: "a bug wearing the costume of an optimization" | Performs wit instead of stating the relation | Say it plainly: "an authorization bug, not a performance fix" | An image that carries meaning in a requested creative or personal piece |
| Repeated significance: "This matters. It's the key. That changes everything." | Repeats one proposition | Keep the sentence that contributes a concrete implication | Separate consequences, deliberate anaphora, or a necessary reminder |
| Synonym cycling: product/platform/ecosystem/solution | Makes one referent look like several objects | Repeat the accurate noun | Distinct entities that really require different terms |
| Fake or vague specificity: "I learned this last Tuesday," "a package once caused a problem" | Invents credibility, or has the grammar of an example with the content of an abstraction | Restore supplied facts; ask which one, when, and how it was caught; or mark a `[TK]` | A supplied event or clearly framed fiction |
| Mechanical shape: every section opens with a definition, every example is interpreted, every paragraph ends on a quotable line | Makes every thought sound prepackaged | Fix the dominant shape only; follow this argument's development | Steps, parallel comparisons, checklists, or a chosen poetic pattern |
| Symmetrical hedging or tidy false balance | Hides a view the author holds | State the view and give the strongest real objection its own space | Deliberately neutral reporting or a genuinely open question |
| Forced empathy: "I know exactly how you feel" | Claims an experience or knowledge the speaker may not have | Acknowledge the stated situation and respond concretely | A supplied shared experience; the user's deliberate wording |
| Empty closer: "The future belongs to those who act," or a recap of the paragraph | Repeats significance and delays the ending | Stop, or use the actual next step or implication | A requested rallying speech or a fitting artistic ending |
| Overpolished neutrality | Removes a real preference, feeling, or boundary | Preserve the user's position and honest texture | Deliberate neutral reporting or diplomatic phrasing that keeps the decision |
| Assistant framing in standalone text: "Great question!", "I hope this helps", "Would you like me to…" | Addresses someone who is not the reader | Remove it | A sincere greeting or sign-off in a real letter or reply |
| Gap covered with a guess: "details are not publicly available," "likely grew up…," "it is believed that" | Presents absence of information as information | Say what the source does not show, or cut the sentence | An attributed estimate; a real limit the reader needs to know |
| Draft artifacts: an unfilled bracket, citation markup, a tracking parameter in a link | Shows the piece was not finished | Resolve from a source, or tell the user what is missing | An intentional template; a `[TK]` the author still owes |
| Reply that re-explains what the reader knows | Buries the answer under shared history | Lead with the decision; keep only the reasoning that would change whether the reader agrees | A reader who is new to the thread |
| Writing about the document: "the table below compares…"; a heading repeated in its first sentence | Describes the page in place of the subject | Say the thing; let the structure show itself | A caveat that changes how to read the data; long reference documents |
| Announced count: "Five things to know" with padded items | Makes the content fit the number | Give the items the material supports | A list whose count is the content's own |

## Meaning comes before replacements

"The trial suggests a possible improvement" must not become "The trial proves it works." Preserve qualifiers, sample limits, time windows, attribution, units, and conditions. A stronger verb is useful only when it is equally true. Never turn an association into a cause, "may" into "will," "some" into "most," or a vendor's claim into narrator fact just to make a sentence firmer.

"I can't make Friday" must remain a refusal when made warmer. "The target is June, if testing passes" remains a target with a dependency when made bolder. "The preview loads; export fails" does not prove why the export fails. Smoother phrasing cannot supply the missing evidence.

An example, an image, or a precise mechanism can enliven prose when the brief supports it. If facts are sparse, write a proportionately sparse piece. Do not make up numbers or emotional experience as a cure for vagueness. In a requested fictional frame, invention can be appropriate and should remain in that frame.

## Rhythm without a recipe

Read for where the thought moves. Repeated short sentences may conceal a useful relationship; uniformly long sentences may overload the reader. Fix those concrete failures. Do not mechanically alternate sentence lengths, force a long sentence into every paragraph, manufacture fragments, or insert an aside to create supposed human variation.

A confirmation can be "Tuesday works." A careful comparison may need a long sentence. Technical steps can remain parallel. A lyric passage may use repetition intentionally. No cadence statistic decides among them.

## Keep ordinary words and useful structures

There is no banned vocabulary. "Robust" may be the precise statistical term; "framework" may name a real software artifact; "I hope" can express an actual wish; "thank you" can do relational work. Replacing these words with less accurate synonyms can make the text worse. When a word is doing no work, a plainer choice usually exists, but keep the original whenever the plain word is less accurate:

| Often doing no work | Plainer choice |
|---|---|
| delve into | look at |
| tapestry, landscape, realm | name the actual thing |
| underscore, leverage (verb), utilize | show, use |
| seamless, robust (outside statistics) | say how it is easy or reliable |
| crucial, pivotal | important, or say why |
| foster, elevate, unlock | build, improve, enable |
| prior to, is able to, a number of | before, can, some or the number |

There is no universal punctuation fingerprint. Em dashes, colons, curly quotes, exclamation marks, parentheses, emojis, and headings can fit. Follow the user's explicit constraints and the piece's context. Keep exact quotations, code, proper names, addresses, mathematical notation, and required house style intact.

A three-item list may contain exactly three necessary things, and two items are right when two is true. A genuine contrast, rhetorical question, repeated noun, or first-person statement can be right. Inspect function and meaning before applying any pattern label.

## What to keep

Removing these is the over-correction failure, because they are voice rather than formula: an earned fragment after a long sentence, deliberate parallelism that makes a real distinction, a closing line the piece earned, first-person conviction where the author owns a position, a real aside or self-correction, a mixed feeling or unresolved doubt, deliberate repetition of the right ordinary word, trade language, and an unusual checkable detail. The test is whether the author could defend the choice if asked. Never insert any of these as "human signals": existing texture can be preserved, but invented texture is performance.

## Calibrate the touch

Use a light touch for a personal piece or an author with a strong style: fix clear formula and protect voice. Use a heavier touch for corporate and marketing copy, which tends to collect stock language, while staying surgical. Match register: a playful piece stays playful and a white paper stays measured; do not neutralize every piece toward a generic middle.

## Match the requested tone without performing it

- Warmth works through a real acknowledgment, an appropriate invitation, or useful care. It does not need repeated reassurance.
- Analytical writing earns its structure with evidence and reasoning. It does not need a pros-and-cons grid or a summary of what it will explain.
- Boldness makes a position clear. It does not require "undeniably," a universal claim, or an aggressive slogan.
- Playfulness uses timing and an apt surprise. It does not require a pun, joke, and emoji in every paragraph.
- Expertise appears in precise distinctions and audience-aware explanation. It does not require named credentials or unnecessary jargon.
- Plainness removes friction. It does not erase nuance, personal feeling, artistic choices, or useful detail.

## Assessment and stopping

Use the format in `review.md`: piece-level diagnosis first, then one block per material issue with a keep, revise, ask-author, or cut verdict. Quote a short exact passage, name the observable issue, and explain the reader consequence or a concrete edit. Group overlapping findings. If the only objection is that a word is on someone's AI-tell list, reconsider the finding. If the passage is effective as written, say so.

A rewrite must not reproduce the pattern it removed under different punctuation: a dash pivot that becomes a colon pivot, or a staged contrast split into two short sentences, is the same move. Repair the claim rather than the punctuation. The same holds for a synonym standing where the flagged word was, clipped fragments, and a sharper slogan in place of the old one; `preservation.md` lists the habits a fix must not create.

Do not produce an authenticity percentage or declare that a text will pass detectors. Do not paraphrase repeatedly until all linter candidates disappear. Stop when the requested result is clear, accurate, and suited to its reader.
