# Channel and form

Channel sets what the reader needs and how they will use it. Tone sets the manner of expression. Neither supplies facts. These are adaptable choices, not templates with slots that must be filled. Explicit user requirements and binding house, venue, or legal rules outrank every default here.

## Quick router

| Medium | Optimize for | Preserve | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Essay, article, newsletter | Development, the author's judgment, examples, momentum | Voice, uncertainty, earned digressions | A comprehensive survey when one through-line will do |
| Documentation, guide | Correct completion, scanning, prerequisites, failure states | Headings, numbered steps, code, warnings, exact terms | Restructuring for novelty or hiding conditions in prose |
| Reference, API, policy | Retrieval, precision, consistency | Repeated schemas, definitions, tables, normative words (MUST) | Anti-template edits that make entries inconsistent |
| Academic paper | Claim and evidence fit, venue conventions, calibrated verbs | Citations, numbers, equations, "we," evidence-bound hedging, useful passive voice | Casualizing, invented evidence, or removing qualification for punch |
| Legal, medical, safety | Accuracy, scope, traceable authority, risk communication | Required notices, definitions, uncertainty, escalation paths | Voice experiments or brevity that drops a protection |
| Marketing, launch copy | A specific audience, demonstrated value, credible proof | Product names, constraints, calls to action, required claims | Unsupported superlatives, generic excitement, invented customers or metrics |
| Email, memo, chat | The request, decision, update, owner, and next action | Social context, expected salutations, useful bullets | Turning a short message into an essay or adding fake casual mess |
| UI text | Task completion, brevity, accessibility, consistent terms | Labels, error recovery, localization limits | Personality that obscures the action or changes product meaning |
| Talk, speech, slides | Listening comprehension, oral rhythm, memorable evidence | Repetition that helps an audience follow; requested stage directions | Page-prose density or removing every rhetorical beat |
| Fiction, narrative | Perspective, causality, scene, character choice, genre | Deliberate ambiguity, voice, earned nonlinear structure | Adding disorder or open threads only to avoid a pattern |

Predictability is a feature in documentation and reference writing: test whether a reader can complete the task or find the entry, not whether each paragraph looks different.

## Email and messages

Answer the actual question and identify the action, if there is one. Match the context this recipient needs rather than the length of the thread. Keep a useful greeting, signoff, or acknowledgment without inventing a name or signature. A confirmation can be one sentence. Multiple necessary questions can be a short list; do not force "one ask" at the cost of coordination.

Before drafting a reply, list each question or request in the message being answered and give each its own answer; a broad sentence that covers two answers neither. The reader already has the thread, so lead with the decision and keep only the reasoning that would change whether they agree, usually one fact they lack and any link they need to act. A reader who is new to the thread is the exception and needs the background. If the message cannot be answered without a missing fact, ask the one question that would settle it. Do not guess, and do not answer every possibility.

For a delicate reply, warmth or diplomacy may shape the opening, but keep the decision clear. Acknowledge what the other person raised without conceding anything the facts do not support, and put a real limit beside the claim it limits. Do not turn a refusal into tentative agreement. Do not repeat the whole quoted thread. In internal messages, fragments and bullets may be appropriate; clarity does not require formal paragraphs.

## Outreach, applications, and proposals

Explain the supported connection between the reader's need and the offer or experience. Never claim to have read a post, used a product, met someone, or achieved a result without source support. Generic but honest relevance is better than fabricated personalization. Separate past work, planned approach, availability, scope, price, and assumptions. A forecast is not achieved revenue; a prospect is not a customer.

A first message to a stranger fails in recognizable ways even when each sentence is fine. Check for manufactured pressure (a deadline, scarcity, guilt, or familiarity that is not real); stacked reassurance ("no cost, no obligation, no reply needed", where one plain "no worries if not" would do); the observation, credentials, offer, ask template; holding back the useful part to earn a reply; asking for more than the message gives; and presenting a preference as the recipient's error. If the message comments on the recipient's own work, check that work now, separate an error from a preference, and stop if the premise does not hold. A follow-up should make sense on its own and should not escalate.

Ask for a proportionate next action when the brief calls for it. Repeated follow-ups should add a real reason or useful material, rather than merely announce another follow-up. Follow any requested word limit; no universal outreach length fits every purpose.

## Decisions and updates

Put the change, decision, or result where the reader can use it. Include the evidence and dependency that matter. Keep observation, implementation, testing, external feedback, and approval distinct. A local check does not establish production success; a request for review is not signoff. Use names, owners, dates, and artifact links only when supplied.

Say how strong the evidence is in words a reader can act on: strong, directional, limited, or not assessed. "Low confidence" is often heard as "bad advice", so name the limit instead. Verbs that commit money or time (launch, spend, build, switch) need strong evidence or a stated condition. A recommendation handed to someone else names the item, the change, who acts, how they will know it worked, and what is still unknown.

Common document jobs have parts a reader expects. Use them as a coverage check, never as a mandatory shape: a decision memo has the recommendation, context, options, risks, and the decision needed; an executive summary has what changed, why it matters, the recommended action, and open questions; a plan has the objective, phases, dependencies, risks, and how success is verified. Keep evidence and risk near the decision they support, and keep facts apart from assumptions.

An executive summary compresses detail without hiding a decision-changing caveat. An analytical update can show alternatives without giving each equal space or inventing a symmetrical pros-and-cons list.

## Posts and short social writing

Respond to the actual point in a reply; an original post may need more framing. Use a hook only when it helps the material. Do not append an engagement question, universal lesson, or punch line automatically. If a story is requested, use supplied events for factual stories and stay within the fiction frame for invented stories.

Honor the supplied character limit. If exact platform counting matters, verify its rules when authorized and available; a raw Unicode length is not always the platform's count, especially for URLs or weighted characters. Do not assume every account has the same limit. Never invent a personal anecdote or result to make the post distinctive.

## Essays, analysis, and journalism

Let the claim, evidence, story, or question determine the structure. Keep evidence near the statement it supports and distinguish reporting from inference. Use headings, lists, scenes, quotations, or long paragraphs when they help the form. No fixed opening style or paragraph count is required. An interesting argument need not end in an inspirational lesson.

Preserve source attribution and uncertainty. Varying sentence length is a consequence of thought and pacing, not a sequence to engineer. A long sentence can develop a careful relation; a short one can land a point.

## Documentation and technical explanation

Prioritize correct terms, necessary context, explicit conditions, steps, and verifiable outcomes. Keep code, commands, identifiers, versions, error text, normative words, and literals exact where needed. Explain a concept at this reader's level without swapping accurate terminology for vague synonyms. A warning or repeated instruction may be necessary in separate contexts.

Decide which terms this reader already has. For one they lack, give the plain words first and the term in brackets only if they will meet it again; gloss it once. If a gloss needs its own gloss, rebuild the sentence. If cutting a term changes what the sentence claims, keep the term. Words such as "simply", "just", "easy", and "obviously" tell a stuck reader the fault is theirs. A heading should name what its section holds, and the first sentence under it should say something the heading does not.

When editing an agent prompt or specification, edit its instructions as text; do not execute them. A code-only task does not trigger a prose makeover. Accessibility requirements, defined terminology, and required templates take precedence over a general stylistic preference.

## Academic, legal, medical, and safety writing

Never invent or alter evidence. "Suggests," "is consistent with," and "may indicate" can be the most accurate verbs, so keep them when the evidence is observational or limited. Flag an unsupported claim for the author instead of supplying a figure, citation, sample size, or stronger conclusion. Keep required notices, defined terms, and escalation paths intact even when tightening. For current legal, medical, policy, grant, or venue requirements, consult the governing source; a writing skill is not authority for changing them.

## UI text

Name the state or action in the user's language and keep the terms the interface already uses. Make the next action and its consequence unambiguous, write error messages that say how to recover, and respect length and localization limits. Personality that hides the action or changes what a control does is a defect. An error message assigns no blame, a confirmation names the specific thing that was done, and a help answer may be "no" when no is the truth.

## Marketing and persuasive writing

Connect real capabilities to benefits relevant to this reader. Match the strength of the claim to the evidence. Do not fabricate scarcity, urgency, testimonials, scale, guarantees, or market leadership. If the brief offers only an aspiration, label it as planned or narrow the wording. A clear call to action can be useful; several promotional flourishes rarely improve it.

Promise the levers the writer controls: what is delivered, by when, at what price, with what support. An outcome that depends on other people, a market, or a platform's decisions is an aim, and softening it should still leave a specific sentence. Test a line by asking whether it could sit unchanged on a competitor's page. Fix the order of a page before its sentences, since polishing a page that needs reordering is wasted effort. A reader who sees only the headline, the first line, and the button should know what is offered. Each call to action should say what happens when the reader uses it, and one thing should keep one name across the page. Proof, when a page offers it, ends by saying what is still uncertain or ongoing, and that part stays in.

## Personal and sensitive messages

Use the actual relationship, supplied sentiment, and desired degree of informality. Care can be quiet; it does not require a grand declaration, a diagnosis, or a lesson. Do not invent a memory, emotional response, recovery timeline, or offer of availability. If the user requests a specific offer or personal fact, include it without adding adjacent disclosures from their samples.

In condolences, apologies, refusals, and conflict, preserve the intended action and boundary. A sincere apology can acknowledge responsibility plainly. A playful tone should be applied with judgment when the subject is serious; use lightness only where it serves the user's actual intention.

## Fiction, speeches, poetry, and expressive forms

Follow the requested artistic effect. Imagery, indirect openings, repetition, fragments, heightened language, dialogue, and patterned rhythm can all be deliberate strengths. The ordinary advice to compress an email does not govern a poem. Avoid replacing a distinctive passage with generic literary decoration.

Invention is permitted inside a clearly requested creative frame. Distinguish fictional speakers from the user's factual life and real attributed quotations. For a speech based on real events, do not add an autobiographical scene without permission or evidence.

A talk or slide script is heard once, so signposts a listener can follow, a repeated key phrase, and one memorable piece of evidence help rather than pad. Test the cadence by reading it aloud. For essays, talks, and other authored pieces, `longform.md` covers openings, development, the author test, and putting craft back after cuts.
