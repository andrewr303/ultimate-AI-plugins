# Authored long-form

Use for essays, articles, newsletters, talks, opinion pieces, narrative, and other authored writing. It adds positive craft after the subtraction pass. It never overrides factual fidelity, the author's supplied voice, the medium, or the brief.

## Decide what the reader carries away

Picture one reader and separate what they already know from what they still need; that gap sets the scope. Choose one takeaway suited to the register: an argument needs a position someone could dispute, an explanation needs a mechanism the reader can follow, an evocation needs an image or feeling it can honestly produce, a narrative needs events and a reason to continue, and a talk needs a thought a listener can keep without rereading. Do not force a thesis onto a piece whose job is wonder, remembrance, description, or story.

## Make each sentence pay

Read each sentence against the one before it. It should add a fact, image, mechanism, example, qualification, consequence, or a useful change in pressure. Cut throat-clearing, duplicated explanation, and any conclusion that exists only because the form seems to expect one, and let the piece be as short as its job allows.

Prefer the plain accurate word. Break a sentence when it asks the reader to hold unrelated ideas at once or loses its subject, but keep a long sentence when one visible subject carries its clauses and the accumulation builds momentum. Length is a prompt to listen, not a verdict.

## Give the opening one job

The first sentence should make the intended reader want the second while starting the actual work. A supported fact, number, scene, image, real question, or claim can do that; a definition, a historical panorama, an announcement of intent, or a summary of the topic usually cannot. Never manufacture a startling fact or scene for a lead. If the source contains no honest opening material, start plainly or ask.

## Make the piece develop

Each paragraph should answer something the previous one raised, complicate it, show it, or make the next one necessary. If paragraphs can trade places without loss, the piece is a list dressed as paragraphs. Name the relation at a join that carries the argument (because, although, once, if, so, which) when adjacency only imitates reasoning, and keep a clean juxtaposition when sequence, surprise, or speed is the point.

For an argument, state the author's leaning and give the strongest real objection enough room to matter, then answer or concede it. Never invent a weak opponent to defeat. Other registers do not owe a debate.

## The author test

For each paragraph, ask whether it could appear nearly word for word in someone else's piece on the same subject. If it could, it is filler, however well made. What survives the test is the author's own material: observed details, measured numbers, incidents they were part of, arguments they lived through, and beliefs they changed. A draft without that material has a sourcing problem rather than a prose problem, so report the gap and offer the interview in `references/interview.md` instead of disguising it with polish.

## Keep the author in the prose

Let supplied samples and language set vocabulary, formality, person, punctuation, and rhythm, with explicit preferences ranking above both. Ask whether the author would comfortably say the sentence to this reader. That test catches borrowed erudition, fake humility, and formality with no purpose; it does not require every sentence to sound like a transcript. Protect real asides, mixed feelings, trade language, deliberate repetition, jokes, and odd details, and never insert them as evidence of humanness.

## Put craft back

Subtraction alone leaves colder prose. After cutting, inspect the passage that needs the most weight:

- **Image:** can the reader see, hear, or feel anything? Sensory and verifiable specificity are different; use the kind the piece needs and the source supports.
- **Stance:** what does the author want, prefer, fear, enjoy, doubt, or refuse? Neutrality can be right in reference prose, but in authored work it should be a choice rather than residue.
- **Rhythm:** let syntax carry a relation or an experience, never missing reasoning.
- **Warmth:** restore humor, curiosity, generosity, unease, or delight that the draft had and the edit erased.
- **Memorable line:** keep one the preceding material earned, and flatten one that performs a depth the piece did not build.

When craft needs material the source lacks, ask or leave `[TK: question]` rather than inventing an image, attitude, or reaction.

## Rewrite, listen, stop

Real rewriting may move the third paragraph to the top, merge two sections, drop the writer's favorite passage, or uncover the plain sentence inside a polished one, within the scope the user authorized. Smoothing every rough edge is not rewriting when the rough edge carries the thought.

Read the finished piece once, aloud or in your head, and fix what obstructs the reader: plodding paragraphs, breathless clauses, repeated sentence shapes, abrupt changes of footing, and words the author would never say. Then fix the single weakest remaining material issue and stop, because repeated polishing passes converge on a flat, uniform register.
